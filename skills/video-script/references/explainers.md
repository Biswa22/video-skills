# Explainers

An explainer shows how something works: a technology, a process, a body part. Its body is a **problem chain**: each mechanism is the answer to a problem the previous answer created, so the viewer always knows why the next part exists. Facts placed side by side, each correct, read as a list rather than a story; the chain is what makes them a story.

## Shape

1. **Mystery.** Open on something the viewer uses or sees every day, and name what seems impossible about it. A framing device (an x-ray view, a zoom into the device, slowed-down time) is introduced once. From then on the visuals carry it and the narration talks about the subject.
2. **First mechanism.** The simplest true account of what happens.
3. **Chain.** Each next beat opens on the problem the last answer causes, spoken as a turn ("But that creates a new problem", "But there's a catch") followed by the question the viewer would now ask. State the problem in full, including why the obvious fix fails, before giving the answer. Plant the premise of the next problem inside the current answer: say that both devices jump channels together before asking how the second device knows where to jump.
4. **Recap.** One sentence that runs through every mechanism in order and ends on the viewer's experience from the opening.

**Recap test:** every body beat appears in the recap. A beat the recap doesn't need is a detour, however true or interesting, so cut it or rewrite the recap to need it. Flashbacks ("rewind to the moment you...") are usually detours; tell the chain forward in time.

## Lines written for pictures

The narration is half of the telling; the picture is the other half. Write each line so its picture follows from it, then list the picture beside it.

- Each line names something that can be drawn (a wave, a channel, a box, a person) and the action the picture shows.
- Replace figures nobody can picture (2.4 GHz, 1 Mbps) with what they look like or do: "a narrow slice of radio", "a million every second". Counts the screen can show, such as 79 channels or 1,600 jumps a second, stay in.
- Use the real term when the picture can define it. "A slightly higher frequency for a one", over a wave whose peaks tighten, teaches the word; a metaphor that stands in for the term ("ripples bunch closer") has to be undone the next time the term matters.
- A concrete setting (a crowded train) and a recurring character (an eavesdropper sitting on it) make an abstract threat visible, and the character returns in each beat the threat touches.
- One word per thing. When two things could share a word, such as the key that opens a lock and the code that scrambles the data, give them different words and keep them for the whole script.
- When a curious viewer would ask something at that moment (why can't I see it? how does it get through a wall?), answer it in a clause and move on.

## Analogies

- Choose the analogy whose steps match the real mechanism step for step. Paint mixing maps onto Diffie–Hellman key exchange, which is how most modern pairing works; a padlock sent open maps onto public-key transport, a different protocol. A simplification drops a detail of the real process; it never swaps in a neighbouring process.
- Name the one-way property the mechanism depends on ("you can't unmix paint").
- Close the analogy with one line mapping it back to the real thing ("instead of colours, the devices use very large numbers").

## Tone and accuracy

- Write for a smart viewer from outside the field: the full mechanism in plain words, with the explanation itself as the interest. Keep jokes to moments that don't carry the explanation.
- History, inventors and the origin of a name enter only if a later beat needs them.
- From a reference video, borrow structure (the order of questions, a recurring visual metaphor) and never wording. Check its facts against primary sources: popular explainers often get the mechanism itself wrong.

## Length

Runtime follows the chain: roughly 30 to 45 seconds per mechanism, plus the opening and the recap. Four mechanisms run about 2.5 to 3 minutes, around 500 to 600 spoken words at 3 words per second. The 30-to-60-second default in the checklist is for single-idea videos.
